    The principles

    Attacking a set defence

    They lifted, they split, they are balanced and waiting. This is the position you asked about, and the first thing to accept is that your smash is not supposed to win the rally. The pair hitting down has the initiative; your job is to force a reply weak enough that your front player finishes it. Attack is a sequence, not a shot.

    • The middle seam is the default. When both defenders are covering their outside lanes, the middle is the biggest gap — and a shuttle down the seam removes their angles and creates a moment of hesitation between them.
    • The racket-side hip is the body target. For a right-hander that is the right hip, which sits on the inside of their body — so against a right-handed pair the body smash and the seam are nearly the same ball. Jamming there cramps the swing and forces a forehand/backhand decision they do not want.
    • Placement beats pace. A half smash landing just inside the tramline does more damage than a full-pace smash hit straight at somebody's racket.
    • Straight before cross. The cross smash is the longest ball on the court: it gives the defender the most time, gives you the least, and drags your front player out of the position that makes your attack work.
    • From a deep corner, do not smash. Off balance and behind the shuttle, a full smash arrives slow and flat — exactly what a set defence wants. Half smash or drop, keep the attack, get your feet back.
    • Change the tempo, not the pace. The third smash in a row is the one they are grooved for. That is when the sliced drop into a front corner is worth more than anything hit hard.
    • Hit where the defender just left. When one of them comes forward, the hole behind them is open for exactly one shot. Take it.

    Defending the smash

    The trigger is simple and it is shared: lift, release the net, split, and be balanced before their racket contacts the shuttle. It is not a memorised clockwise rotation — it is a habit both players run at the same moment.

    • The straight block is the backbone. Shortest distance, least that can go wrong, and if it is tight they cannot hit down off it.
    • The push into the mid-court is the shot that breaks them. They are in front-and-back, so the space behind their net player is the largest hole on their court. A flat push into it forces a lift and flips the rally — this is the professional answer, not just survival.
    • The cross block is the most intercepted shot in doubles. It travels slowly through exactly the zone their front player is standing in.
    • Drive only when you have height. Against a steep smash you will be contacting below the tape, and the shuttle goes straight up. Against a slow, flat smash from the baseline, drive it down the line and you have taken the attack.
    • Body smashes go on the backhand. Racket up and in front, thumb behind the grip, short punch, no swing. A jammed defender who tries to swing pops it up.
    • Lift deep, or not at all. A lift keeps you defending; a short lift makes their next smash steeper. Use it when you are stretched, not by default.

    Shape

    • Side-by-side to defend, front-and-back to attack. Two players in a line cannot cover the width against a pair hitting down; two players side by side cannot finish a rally.
    • The one who lifted takes their own side. They retreat down the line of their lift, the partner balances into the other half.
    • The front player follows the line of the smash. The straight block is the most likely reply to any smash — stand on it before it is played.
    • Both defenders move as one. Side-by-side is not two statues; when the shuttle goes to one side, the whole pair slides that way and the gap between you never opens.
    • Follow a good block in. The player who blocks takes the net, the partner slides behind. A block you admire from the mid-court is a block you wasted.

    Reading the hands

    A left-hander moves every target on the court, and most players never check. Facing you, a right-hander's racket is on your left; a left-hander's is on your right.

    • Right-hander on your left + left-hander on your right = two backhands in the middle. The seam is the softest target in doubles and it is there all rally.
    • Left-hander on your left + right-hander on your right = two forehands in the middle. Leave the seam alone and attack the lines.

    What this trainer can and cannot do

    It builds recognition and decision speed — seeing the shape and knowing the answer before the shuttle arrives. That is genuinely half the problem, and it is the half you can train on a phone. The other half is your body producing the shot under pressure, and that only comes from multi-shuttle feeds and half-court drills with a partner. Use this to know what you are trying to do; use the court to make it automatic.
