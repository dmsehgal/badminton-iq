How this works

How to read the court
  • You are always at the bottom, looking towards the net. Every instruction is written from that view, so "your left" means the left of the picture.
  • Targets are numbered. Tap a number to place your shot. The coaching afterwards refers to those same numbers, so there is never any confusion about whose left is whose.
  • A solid ring is a place on the court. A dashed ring is a target on a person — aiming at the hip they hold their racket next to, so they have no room to swing.
  • The white pip on a player shows their racket side. Facing you, a right-hander's racket is on your left. That single fact moves several targets, which is what the last level is about.
Words used on the way
How levels unlock
  • Score 60% on a level to open the next one. Your best result on each position counts, so replaying a level to fix the ones you missed moves you forward.
  • The decision drill opens once you have passed a level, and only ever asks about levels you have unlocked.
  • Every finished run goes on the leaderboard — one board per level, plus an overall table that adds your best run of each level together.

        The principles

        Attacking when they have lifted and spread out

        They have lifted, they have spread out side-by-side, and they are balanced and waiting. The first thing to accept is that your smash is not supposed to win the rally. The pair hitting downwards has the advantage; your job is to force a reply weak enough that your partner at the net can finish it. Attacking is a sequence, not a single shot.

        • The gap between them is the default target. When they are each covering their own side, the middle is the biggest space — and a shuttle down the middle takes away their angles and creates a moment where neither is sure whose ball it is.
        • Aim at the hip on their racket side. For a right-hander that is the right hip, which sits on the inside of their body — so against a right-handed pair, jamming the body and hitting the middle are almost the same ball. There is no room to swing there, and they have to choose forehand or backhand with no time to do it.
        • Placement beats power. A three-quarter-power smash landing just inside the sideline does more damage than a full-power one hit straight at somebody's racket.
        • Straight before diagonal. A smash played diagonally is the longest shot on the court: it gives the defender the most time, gives you the least, and drags your partner away from the net, which is the position that makes your attack work at all.
        • From a back corner, do not smash. Off balance and not properly behind the shuttle, a full smash arrives slow and flat — exactly what a settled defence wants. Play three-quarter power or a drop, keep the attack, and get your feet back.
        • Change the speed, not the power. The third smash in a row is the one they are ready for. That is when a sliced drop into a front corner is worth more than anything hit hard.
        • Hit where the player just left. When one of them comes forward, the space behind them is open for exactly one shot. Take it.

        Defending the smash

        The habit is simple and both of you run it at once: lift, leave the net, spread out, and be balanced before their racket meets the shuttle. It is not a rotation to memorise — it is a shared trigger.

        • Taking the pace off, straight back, is the backbone. Shortest distance, least that can go wrong, and if it lands tight to the net they cannot hit down off it.
        • The flat shot into the middle of their court is what actually breaks them. They are in a front-and-back shape, so the space behind their net player is the biggest empty area on their court. A firm flat shot into it forces a lift and flips the rally — this is the shot good players use, not just survival.
        • Playing it diagonally across the net is the most intercepted shot in doubles. It travels slowly through exactly the area their net player is standing in.
        • Only hit back flat when you have the height. Against a steep smash you are contacting below the top of the net and the shuttle goes straight up. Against a slow, flat smash from the baseline, hit it back flat down the line and you have taken the attack.
        • Take body smashes on the backhand. Racket up and in front, thumb behind the grip, short punch, no swing. A jammed player who tries to swing pops it up.
        • Lift deep, or not at all. A lift keeps you defending; a short one makes their next smash steeper. Use it when you are stretched, not as a default.

        Where to stand

        • Side-by-side to defend, one-in-front-of-the-other to attack. Two players in a line cannot cover the width against a pair hitting down; two players side-by-side cannot finish a rally.
        • Whoever lifted takes their own side. They back away down the line of their own lift, and the partner takes the other half.
        • The net player follows the line of the smash. The most likely reply to any smash comes straight back down the same line — stand on it before it is played.
        • Both defenders move as one. Side-by-side is not two people standing still; when the shuttle goes to one side, the whole pair slides that way and the gap between you never opens.
        • Follow a good defensive shot forward. Whoever played it takes the net, the partner slides in behind. A good block admired from the middle of your own court is a good block wasted.

        Left-handers

        A left-hander moves every target on the court, and most players never check. Facing you, a right-hander's racket is on your left; a left-hander's is on your right.

        • Right-hander on your left, left-hander on your right: both rackets point away from the middle, so the gap between them is covered by two backhands. That is the softest target in doubles and it is there all rally.
        • Left-hander on your left, right-hander on your right: both rackets point into the middle. Leave the gap alone and attack the sidelines.

        What this trainer can and cannot do

        It builds recognition and decision speed — seeing the shape and knowing the answer before the shuttle arrives. That is genuinely half the problem, and it is the half you can train on a phone. The other half is your body producing the shot under pressure, and that only comes from feeding drills and half-court practice with a partner. Use this to know what you are trying to do; use the court to make it automatic.
